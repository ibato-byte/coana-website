Starting a company in Japan is one challenge. Keeping its books in order, in a system designed for Japanese speakers, is another. Many foreign founders we meet are confident about their business, but unsure how bookkeeping works here and who is responsible for what.

This guide explains **who does what, which tools to choose, the Japanese invoicing customs to know, and a monthly routine you can run in English**, with a checklist and common mistakes at the end.

## The short answer

You can run your Japanese company's books in English if you do three things from day one:

1. **Know which tasks belong to a licensed professional**, and keep everything else in your own hands (or your bookkeeping support's)
2. **Choose cloud tools that work for you** and connect them to your bank and cards
3. **Set simple rules before your first invoice**, and follow a fixed monthly routine

Tax rules in Japan change from time to time, and how they apply depends on your company. **For tax decisions, always check with your licensed tax accountant (zeirishi).**

In Japan, some tasks are legally reserved for licensed professionals.

- **You (or your bookkeeping support)**: recording transactions, issuing invoices, tracking payments, organizing receipts, monthly reports
- **Licensed tax accountant (zeirishi)**: tax filings and tax advice
- **Labor and social security attorney (sharoushi)**: social insurance and labor insurance procedures
- **Judicial scrivener (shiho-shoshi)**: company registration and changes to it
- **Administrative scrivener (gyosei-shoshi)**: many business permits and licenses

Knowing this split early saves time. Your daily bookkeeping can be organized in English, while filings are prepared by professionals who work from the same data. ## Choose cloud accounting tools that work in English

Japanese cloud accounting software connects directly to Japanese banks and credit cards, which removes most manual entry. When choosing tools, check:

- Whether the interface or support is available in English, or whether a bilingual partner can set it up for you
- Whether it connects to your bank accounts and cards
- Whether it can store receipts and invoices as data, linked to each transaction
- Whether your tax accountant can access the same data

Set up the account structure once, carefully, and the daily work becomes much simpler. ## Japanese invoicing customs to know

  <figcaption>Figure 2: A typical B2B invoice and payment timeline in Japan</figcaption>

A few customs surprise many founders:

- **Closing date and payment terms**: invoices are often issued at month-end, with payment due at the end of the following month. Terms vary, so agree on them in writing
- **Bank transfers**: most B2B payments are made by bank transfer. Who pays the transfer fee is a common question, so agree on it before the first deal
- **Company seals (hanko)**: some documents still use a company seal, though digital invoices are increasingly common
- **Qualified invoices**: under Japan's invoice system, customers who are taxable businesses generally need a qualified invoice from a registered issuer to claim input tax credits. A qualified invoice must include items such as the issuer's registration number and the tax amount by rate. Confirm with your tax accountant whether and when your company should register

The National Tax Agency publishes an English overview of the invoice system:

- Reference: [JAPAN INVOICE SYSTEM (PDF, English) | National Tax Agency](https://www.nta.go.jp/taxes/shiraberu/zeimokubetsu/shohi/keigenzeiritsu/pdf/0024006-039_01.pdf)

Decide your invoice format, numbering, and payment terms before your first deal, and keep them consistent.

## Keep receipts and invoices for the required period

Japanese corporations generally must keep their books and transaction documents, such as receipts, invoices, and contracts, for **7 years** from the day after the filing deadline of each fiscal year. For a fiscal year with a tax loss carried forward, the period is generally **10 years**.

Invoices and receipts you receive electronically, such as PDF invoices sent by email, must be kept as electronic data, not only as printouts, and in a way you can search by date, amount, and business partner. The details, including relief for smaller businesses, are on the National Tax Agency's pages (in Japanese). Ask your tax accountant how they apply to your company.

- Reference: [No.5930 Retention period for books and documents | National Tax Agency (Japanese)](https://www.nta.go.jp/taxes/shiraberu/taxanswer/hojin/5930.htm)
- Reference: [Electronic Books Maintenance Act Q&A, electronic transactions | National Tax Agency (Japanese)](https://www.nta.go.jp/law/joho-zeikaishaku/sonota/jirei/07denshi/index.htm)

## A monthly bookkeeping routine that works in English

1. **Weekly**: photograph receipts and upload them; check that bank and card feeds are connected
2. **Monthly (first week)**: review imported transactions, issue invoices, check unpaid invoices
3. **Monthly (by the 10th)**: review last month's sales, profit, and cash balance
4. **Quarterly**: share data with your tax accountant and discuss anything unusual

Write this routine down in English. If you hire staff later, they can follow the same steps. ## Checklist: is your bookkeeping ready?

- [ ] You know which tasks go to your tax accountant, labor and social security attorney, and judicial scrivener
- [ ] Your company has a business bank account and card, separate from personal ones
- [ ] Your accounting software is connected to that account and card
- [ ] Your tax accountant can access the same accounting data
- [ ] Your invoice format, numbering, and payment terms are decided
- [ ] You have asked your tax accountant whether to register as a qualified invoice issuer
- [ ] Receipts and electronic invoices are saved in one agreed place, as data
- [ ] Your weekly and monthly routine is written down in English

If some items are still open, that is normal. They are the things to sort out first, with your tax accountant or bookkeeping support.

## Common mistakes foreign founders make

### Leaving everything until year-end

If transactions pile up for a year, the books become hard to reconstruct, and questions to your tax accountant arrive all at once. A short monthly routine is much easier than one large catch-up.

### Mixing personal and business payments

Paying business expenses from a personal card or account makes every transaction harder to explain. Open a business account and card early, and use them for business only.

### Printing PDF invoices and deleting the files

Invoices received by email must be kept as data. Save the original files in one agreed folder or in your accounting software, even if you also like to print them.

### Choosing tools before deciding who will use them

A tool your tax accountant cannot access, or one nobody on your team can read, creates extra work. Decide who will enter, review, and file the data, then choose the tools.

## Summary

You can run your Japanese company's books in English if you know who does what, choose tools that connect to your bank and that your tax accountant can access, and set clear rules before your first invoice. Follow a simple weekly and monthly routine, keep electronic invoices as data, and check tax decisions with a licensed professional. The key is to start organized, rather than fix things at year-end.
